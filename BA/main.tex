\section{Introduction}

Insect pests are responsible for significant production losses in agriculture \cite{climate}. However, pesticides threaten the environment by reducing biodiversity \cite{Wan2025}, particularly among insects, including pollinators that support agricultural production \cite{Smith2022PollinatorDeficits}. Overuse of pesticides causes resistance in pests. Additionally, global warming leads to increasing pest populations, which aggravates the problem further \cite{climate}. To address these challenges, the concept of integrated pest management (IPM) was developed. As defined by the Food and Agriculture Organization of the United Nations (FAO), it sets the goal of minimizing the use of pesticides by applying alternative management strategies wherever possible \cite{IPM_FAO, IPM_book}. %For a detailed look into to history and components of IPM compare Messelink and Kruidhof 2019 \cite{IPM_book}. 
IPM is also the legal basis for the use of pesticides in the EU \cite{IPM_EU}, which, among other things, prescribes the monitoring of insect pests when using pesticides. Precise monitoring facilitates the early and targeted use of pesticides for only small infested patches, the use of less harmful pesticides, or alternative ways of control like beneficial insects. Monitoring is usually based on setting up a grid of sticky traps, which are regularly checked for trapped insects. Because the manual counting requires expertise and takes a lot of time, it is not feasible for larger applications \cite{survey2022}. Hence, computer vision-based systems for automated monitoring of insect pests are considered to be part of the solution. Ongoing efforts to automatize insect pest monitoring have more recently adopted the methods of deep learning (see the surveys Teixeira et al. 2023 \cite{survey2023} and Mittal et al. 2024 \cite{survey2024b}). My thesis tests a method to facilitate the implementation of deep learning-based, automated insect pest monitoring.

The dominant approach in pest monitoring in general involves the supervised training of YOLO models. YOLO ("You Only Look Once") is a deep learning architecture that unifies the tasks of object localization and classification into a single step. It was introduced by Redmon et al. in 2016 \cite{redmon2016you} and has been improved on since then in several iterations by different contributors. For greenhouses, specifically designed neural network models have been employed successfully in 2021 \cite{rustia_automatic_2021, li_field_2021}, but the recent studies from 2023 onward use YOLO models on individual datasets \cite{costa_intelligent_2023, zhang_automatic_2023, wang_deep-learning-based_2024, shi_small_2025}. These six papers all report high performance, with mean average precision (mAP) or F1 scores ranging from 0.89 to 0.94.

Main challenges in the implementation are the small size of pests—which, even with high-resolution cameras, occupy only a very tiny fraction of an image—and the high degree of morphological variation among individuals. In less controlled environments additional complications arise from high morphological similarity between different species.
These challenges create a demand for large annotated datasets; however, the collection and labeling of biological data require considerable effort and expertise. As a result, datasets are often small and exhibit other limitations, such as class imbalance. For an overview of these specific challenges, see Teixeira et al. (2023) \cite{survey2023}. As a remedy, the authors recommend semi-supervised learning (SSL), a family of methods for training with only partially labeled data. Despite this, no survey reports applications of SSL in the field of pest monitoring in general and only one study in the context of the greenhouse makes use of SSL methods: Rustia et al. \cite{rustia_online_2021} proposed a self-training pipeline as a supplement to a fully supervised model as early as 2021. While the results were promising, the implementation was highly intricate, involving multi-stage setups with hand-tailored solutions. This complexity may explain why the approach has not been further explored. Outside of the greenhouse, in open-field settings, some efforts were made from 2024 onward, either focusing on a single insect species with more diverse backgrounds or on a set of larger insects \cite{li_mateacher_2024, majewski_improved_2024, zhou_mutual_2024, zhou_perceptive_2025, gomez-zamanillo_semi-supervised_2025}. Another notable paper in this context is Dang et al. (2024) \cite{dang_efficient_2024}, who employ a field dataset of ten larger insect species and perform segmentation without manual annotation by leveraging a grounding language–image model. While extending this approach with classification and adapting it to the smaller insect pests typically found in greenhouses might be feasible, grounding models will not be considered in this study due to their limited efficiency compared to YOLO-based methods. 

Table~\ref{tab:literature-overview} shows an overview of the relevant literature. Because of the differing datasets and metrics, the results are not directly comparable. Also not seen in the table are the pest classes included in the datasets: Only Rustia et. al \cite{rustia_automatic_2021} covered the same pest classes featured in this project. As of now no YOLO model has been trained to identify common greenhouse pests without relying on supervised training. Although working solutions for automatic insect pest monitoring in the greenhouse have been successfully tested, practical deployment stagnates and is hindered by the need for large, manually annotated datasets for new target domains. To my knowledge, there is no adequate method to reduce the amount of labels needed. My goal is therefore an improvement in implementation efficiency by adapting the labeling and training method.

In this thesis, I test the proposition that, given a specifically structured training dataset, a model can be trained using manual labels only for validation and testing. This should be achievable through a combination of image processing and a semi-supervised learning (SSL) method known as self-training. The key requirement is a particular dataset structure: the dataset must be partitioned into subsets, where each subset contains only one pest class as the foreground objects.
\looseness=1
As a reference point, a supervised YOLO model is trained using manually created labels. It is expected to achieve at least 0.9 precision and 0.8 recall per class. Precision is prioritized over recall, because recall can later be improved by increasing the density of sticky traps, whereas precision depends primarily on model quality. Furthermore, low precision risks unnecessary pesticide application, which would conflict with the principles of IPM.

The proposed automated labeling and SSL workflow consists of two stages. First, bounding boxes for the pest classes are generated using segmentation algorithms. Because the images are pre-sorted to contain only one pest class, the detected bounding boxes can be directly associated with the correct label. In the second stage, the resulting automatically labeled datasets are used to self-train a YOLO model. A more detailed overview of the procedure follows in the next section.

The main goal of the experiments was to test whether a model trained without manual labels could achieve results comparable to those of a model trained on manual labels. \subsection{Data}
\label{sec:data}

\subsubsection{Dataset description}
\label{sec:data_description}
The data was provided by the Julius-Kühn-Institut (JKI) and was collected as part of the Smart-Checkpots project \cite{raaz_smart_2023} where it was labeled manually and used to train a supervised model. The images are divided into four subsets, each associated with only one of four insect pest classes (see Table 2) to simplify the task of manual labeling. Every image in a subset is supposed to depict several instances of only one pest class trapped on a yellow sticky trap (YST) with black background structures. Finding the optimal colors and contrast to attract specific pest classes is a topic of ongoing research (see e.g. \cite{YST}). Yellow/black was chosen for this dataset because it is a compromise for the considered pests and also an industry standard.

The populated YSTs in this dataset were created at the JKI by breeding the insects to the adult stage in a closed environment (see Figure~\ref{fig:breeding}). No artificial light was used to avoid reflections. The pictures have slightly different view angles and different lighting conditions, some still affected by reflections and some having areas with reduced sharpness.

While the YSTs were being populated, some insects were able to leave a trap and were subsequently captured by a neighboring trap. Therefore, the subsets can contain a small number of insects belonging to other subclasses. The tapping method used for the whiteflies also collected parts of dead insects as well as pieces of foliage from the tobacco plants. Thrips were bred on bean plants, which occasionally left considerable amounts of foliage on the YSTs. Furthermore, thrips in larval stages were also trapped, which are difficult to distinguish from male individuals. In the final use case, larvae would not be trapped, as only adults are capable of flight.

For each pest class, a proportion of images were manually labeled by several persons. Visual inspection showed inconsistencies in the correctness of labels, the completeness of labels, and the size of the bounding boxes.

\subsubsection{Preprocessing}

To raise the quality of the dataset, images and labels were checked and improved where possible.

Image files were sorted by their creation time given by the metadata and then the first 100 images for each class were visually inspected, were several images with identical YSTs and different camera settings as leftovers of early experiments with the camera setup were found. These were removed manually. I removed further duplicates (identical images) by comparing file checksums. Images with labels that indicated no insects or unexpected insects not belonging to the class of the subset were also removed. 

For the thrips, I inspected the complete subset before splitting the dataset. This led to the exclusion of 200 images with wrong labels or too many other objects. To improve the quality of the thrips labels further I gave the cut-out image patches given by bounding boxes to an expert from the JKI, which identified 1276 labels pointing to objects other than adult thrips. These labels were removed. Table~\ref{tab:insect-stats} shows the resulting number of images and labels after the cleaning reported here. The dataset is imbalanced, both in terms of the number of labeled images per class and the mean number of instances per image.

The summed difficulties with the thrips images and labels led to exclusion of the class for all experiments but the initial supervised training, which showed that no satisfying results are to be expected in the proposed self-training. There are three major reasons for this decision: low sample size although the difference between males and females demands adequate an number of images, presence of non-target objects as shown by the large number of incorrect labels, and low label quality as shown by the large number of missing labels in the test set. 

Some labels were found to contain negative coordinates. After visual inspection of samples, these values were interpreted as their absolute counterparts, since this matched the actual object positions. The underlying cause of the negative coordinates could not be identified but seems to be connected to the export of labels from Label Studio.

Because the number of insects per images is different for the pest classes (see Table~\ref{tab:insect-stats}) sets were balanced where possible. \subsection{Evaluation}
\label{sec:evaluation}



The evaluation pipeline consists of the following steps: collecting the predictions of one model, filtering the predictions, comparing them to the ground truth, finding the best operating points per class, and finally comparing the models to each other.

The YOLO model used only allows prediction on full images and also only allows one confidence threshold for all classes. I used a wrapper script to enable prediction on tiled images and prediction with class-specific thresholds. For prediction on tiles ($640 \times 640$), a sliding window with a set stride was used to collect the predictions for one image. Strides 420 and 440.

Predictions were filtered by a non-maximum suppression (NMS) filter and another filter to exclude boxes contained inside larger boxes. NMS relies on the intersection-over-union (IoU) metric, which is the ratio of the area of two boxes intersected and the area of their union. When two boxes have an IoU above a threshold, only the box with the higher confidence is kept. However, if a small box is completely or partially inside a larger box, the IoU will be low, because the intersection is only as large as the small box and the union is as large as the large box. As visual inspection shows, the small objects in the dataset make it necessary to include an extra filter for this case. See Figure~\ref{fig:iou_problems}a for false positives that occur without the extra filter. IoU for NMS was set to 0.4 and the threshold for the containment filter was set to 0.5.

The filtered predictions were then sorted by confidence and were attributed to possible ground-truth boxes using greedy matching. Attribution was defined by IoU, but again small boxes made an additional check necessary. Because some manual labels are large, much smaller but correct predictions contained inside these ground-truth boxes produced a low IoU value and had to be attributed with this extra check. See Figure~\ref{fig:iou_problems}b for false positives that occur without the extra comparison. This evaluation logic was also used for comparing the old and new labels for the test set and for evaluating the final results of the image processing. For IoU a threshold of 0.5 was set, and for the extra comparison thresholds between 0.5 and 0.9 were tested.

Results of parameter tuning (IoU thresholds, stride values, and containment thresholds) are summarized in Table~\ref{tab:containment_results}. They were tested on two  models trained on tiles, with the confidence threshold set to 0.5 for all classes, the non-maximum suppression threshold for filtering before comparison to ground truth set to 0.4, and the IoU threshold for comparison to ground truth set to 0.5 in all tests.

To define the quality of a model, per-class precision–recall curves were built, and confidence thresholds that were closest to the target zone of precision greater than or equal to 0.9 and recall greater than or equal to 0.8 were taken as operating points for the model. “Closest” is defined as the orthogonal projection to the boundary of the target zone if the point is left or below it, and as the Euclidean distance to the lower left corner if not. If the curve reached the target zone, the point with the best F1 value inside the zone was chosen. Different models were then compared by the number of operating points inside the target zone and the distance of the remaining operating points to the target zone.


\subsection{Supervised training}
\label{sec:supervised_methods}

As a baseline for the automated labeling process, a YOLOv8 model pretrained on COCO was trained using the provided manual labels. To my knowledge, no task-specific pretrained models were available. The aim of the training was to ascertain that the data was sufficient to reach the target metrics of a minimum per-class precision of 0.9 and recall of 0.8. Since supervised training was not the main focus of this project, experiments were not designed to fully optimize results.

A small YOLOv8s model (released in 2023 by Ultralytics, 11.2 million parameters at 640-pixel images \cite{YOLO8}) was chosen because initial tests showed that larger models or later versions did not considerably improve performance and because the codebase of later models would have been harder to adapt for changes such as the implementation of confidence-based loss weights for pseudo-labels. The main differences to older YOLO models were the feature fusions extracted at different levels to handle objects of different scales, the more refined loss weights, and the large number of included augmentation techniques.

The images were cropped to the size of the YST. This decreased the size of the dataset by 17.1 GB but also introduced different image sizes. Because YOLO-formatted labels are relative to the image size, these had to be recalculated.

Training YOLO with the given image sizes would have required exceedingly large amounts of GPU RAM. Therefore, in the training with full images, these were resized to 1280 pixels. As an alternative, the images were tiled into 640×640 patches for training. For this, the images were padded and then tiled with a stride of 440. The overlap of 200 pixels corresponded to the size of the largest pest class, the fungus gnats, such that an instance was contained in at least one tile. The labels were recalculated according to the tiles and were only kept if at least 80\% of the bounding box lay inside the respective tile. The resulting tiles contained a large number of empty background tiles, which accounted for 68\% of the labeled training set.

Starting from the default settings, short training runs with selected parameter changes were conducted to identify optimization potential, where augmentation settings and the ratio of background to foreground images (only for the tiles) were identified as the main factors influencing performance. Changing the default optimizer (stochastic gradient descent), learning-rate schedule, and loss weights did not yield improvements. Because the dataset already contained considerable variance (see Section~\ref{sec:data_description}), augmentations were applied only subtly. The possible scaling factor was reduced to 30\% to avoid unrealistic size differences between objects, and the probability of mosaic augmentation (introduced in 2020 with YOLOv4 \cite{bochkovskiy2020yolov4}) was lowered to 25\% to prevent the model from learning lighting differences between images. Horizontal flipping was disabled as the images already had mixed orientation. A small amount of mixup \cite{zhang2018mixup} was introduced with a 5\% probability. All other parameters were kept at their default values, which included jitter for all channels of the HSV-encoded image, translation, vertical flips, RandAugment \cite{cubuk2020randaugment}, and random erasing \cite{zhong2020random}.

All models were trained for up to 200 epochs, after which the best epoch (measured by mAP) was selected.

\subsection{Image processing}
\label{sec:imag_proc_methods}
The aim of the image processing is to detect a proportion of objects and create labels including bounding-box coordinates, which can serve as the starting point for the training of a YOLO model. The main idea of this step is to separate background and foreground with a color threshold and then keep all foreground contours as target objects. A rectangle around the contour serves as the bounding box, and the class ID can be derived from the subset of the image, as all insects in an image were supposed to be of the same pest class. For images without background structures, the process would reduce to the short procedure reported in Section~\ref{sec:segmentation}. However, when images contain background structures—as in the dataset used in this study—more complex preprocessing is required to mask out those structures, as detailed in Section~\ref{sec:image_preprocessing}, and augmentation is needed to train the model on occurrences of the target objects in combination with the reintroduced background structures, which is described in Section~\ref{sec:augmentation}.

The presented pipeline was developed with the labeled training set, with the aim of raising the absolute number of true positives and minimizing the number of false positives as the main objectives. Precision was also tracked but considered secondary, as it might have come at the cost of too few true positives. Binarization, which was used at several steps, was achieved through thresholding in the HSV color space, because the hue component provided robustness against variations in lighting. The ranges defined as foreground were: Hue 20–30, Saturation 100–255, and Value 100–255.

\subsubsection{Preprocessing for segmentation}
\label{sec:image_preprocessing}

To filter out background structures, a mask covering these regions was applied to each image. The pixels within the mask were then changed to a yellow hue, allowing them to be identified as background during segmentation. Direct detection of the lines was tested, but it led to no usable results, as the detected line segments were often very short and could not be separated from the objects. Because the images in the dataset were captured from slightly different angles, the mask had to be aligned individually for each image. All image processing was performed using the cv2 Python library.

Before the masking procedure images had to be rotated to have the header of the YST at the top. The required rotation was detected by dividing the YST area into horizontal halves and comparing the summed black pixel amounts in each half, with black pixels defined as having a value below 50. This method became unreliable when the YST was crowded with a large number of black insects, so a final visual inspection was performed to ensure correctness.

Creating the mask required balancing line thickness to fully cover background structures despite minor misalignment, while avoiding excessive coverage of the objects. Binarized and denoised images of empty YSTs, captured under the same conditions as the dataset images, were used for mask generation. The masks were created by combining several empty YST images with projective transformations and then thickening the black structures with dilation (kernel size 25, Figure~\ref{fig:masks}\subref{fig:mb}). From this mask, two variations were generated by applying further dilation (kernel size 50, Figure~\ref{fig:masks}\subref{fig:md}) and by manually thickening the right border lines to cover the holes completely and drawing a rectangle over the header (Figure~\ref{fig:masks}\subref{fig:mf}). For subsequent alignment, the corners of the YST contour and two points of the horizontal midline calculated from the original masks were saved.

After the preparation of the mask and the rotating of the images, preprocessed and masked versions of the image set were created. The pipeline of this process was divided into the following steps:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Find contours on the binarized image and take the largest one, which corresponds to the YST (algorithm used: \texttt{cv2.findContours}, based on \cite{suzuki1985border}).
    \item Assuming a rectangular shape, find the corners (algorithms used: \texttt{cv2.arcLength} and \texttt{cv2.approxPolyDP}, based on the Douglas–Peucker algorithm \cite{douglas1973algorithms}).
    \item Using the corners of the mask and the image, compute a projective transformation to register the mask to the image. Apply this transformation to the mask and to the saved midline points (algorithms used: \texttt{cv2.findHomography} based on RANSAC \cite{fischler1981ransac}, \texttt{cv2.warpPerspective}).
    \item Using the midpoints of the horizontal midlines of the mask and the image, compute an additional affine transformation to further register the mask along the horizontal midline (algorithms used: \texttt{cv2.HoughLines} based on the generalized Hough transform \cite{duda1972use}, \texttt{cv2.perspectiveTransform}, \texttt{cv2.warpAffine}).
    \item Color the pixels indicated by the registered mask in the image yellow to mark them as background.
    \item Crop the image to the YST (and recalculate the manual labels for evaluation).
\end{itemize}


For about 6\% of the images, the corners of the YST could not be detected. These images were dropped.

\subsubsection{Segmentation}
\label{sec:segmentation}

After masking, the images were binarized with the defined thresholds and the foreground objects could be segmented. The results still needed to be filtered to exclude unwanted objects. A first set of filter thresholds was used to evaluate the effects of the different masks. A second, more refined set of thresholds was derived from inspecting the data given by the best-evaluated mask (Figure~\ref{fig:masks}\subref{fig:mf}). Evaluations for different filters were done on the manual labels of the labeled training set. Unlike in the evaluation of model predictions, the comparison of the bounding boxes was reduced to a simple intersection between rectangles with a threshold of 0.5, because it gave nearly identical results for smaller predictions contained in the ground truth and was stricter for overlapping ones.

In the first filter set for comparing the masks, lower and upper thresholds for the detected contour areas were derived from visual inspection (Table~\ref{tab:filter_parameters}, Set 1). The rectangles around the contours were then scaled with a factor of 1.5, because the contours lost small features like legs, which would not have been contained in the rectangles otherwise. Then another threshold was applied to the rectangles. Assuming that more oblong rectangles were likely to be non-insect objects (e.g., leftover background elements, foliage, or parts of insects), the maximum ratio of height to width was set to 2. This excluded rectangles with one side being more than twice as long as the other side.

The results from the best-performing mask (Figure~\ref{fig:masks}\subref{fig:mf}) were used to inspect the distribution of box sizes and box ratios. Additional filter thresholds were then tested on the calculated rectangles and are given in Table~\ref{tab:filter_parameters}, Set 2. The goals were to reduce the number of small insect parts and occasional non-insect objects, as well as the number of large boxes with more than one insect occluding each other. Overlapping boxes posed a problem only for the whiteflies, presumably because individual objects often led to two separate contours. Therefore this case was checked and only the larger rectangle was kept. To adapt to the variations in saturation and value between individual whiteflies, a more intricate binarization as well as additional value thresholding were tested, but did not improve results.

\subsubsection{Augmenting the data}
\label{sec:augmentation}

Masking the background structures excluded all insects on lines or letters and created a biased label set. A model trained exclusively on this dataset would have inherited this bias and learned to ignore insects on background structures. To prevent this, two types of augmentation sets combining insects and the structures were created. The first set distributed background structures randomly on the masked images. The second set started with images of empty sticky traps and distributed cut-out insects on and near the background structures (see Figure~\ref{fig:flowchart_img_processing} for examples).

Specifically, the first augmentation set was created by combining three layers: the background of a masked image, a middle layer containing the prepared background structures from images of empty YSTs, and the foreground layer of the masked image, which contained the insects. The middle layer itself consisted of two layers of background structures that were cut out to remove unwanted parts (border lines, holes) and then rotated and shifted to create variations. This procedure was applied to the complete training set.

The second augmentation set used empty YSTs as the starting point, which were varied in lighting, vertical orientation, and shear. Two different masks were used to define areas on the YST where insects should be placed, ensuring objects were positioned directly on but also near the structures. Cut-out insects were then placed randomly within these areas. For each pest class, 24 images were created.

The insects for the second set were extracted from the original images guided by the bounding boxes created by the method described above. The contours of the individual insects were cut out with binarization using fixed thresholds that were refined as needed. As the detected whitefly contours tended to form loops with empty centers, the pixels inside these loops were identified and also marked as foreground. For fungus gnats and leaf miner flies, individually calculated value thresholds were obtained using Otsu’s method \cite{otsu1979threshold}. Additionally, contours found inside each bounding box were filtered to retain only the largest contour, excluding artifacts. To address a second source of bias in the created labels, value variations were also introduced for the whiteflies. The magnitude of these variations was derived from inspecting the value distribution of true positives and false negatives for the whiteflies, which had a different mean.

\subsection{Self-training}
\label{sec:self_training_methods}
A model was trained on augmented and unaugmented images with the labels created by image processing. In a second step, the model was used to make predicitions on the unaugmented, original images to create pseudo-labels for the insects missed by the mask-based label creation. Taking high-confidence false positive predictions as new labels for further training runs was repeated to improve the label quality further.

\subsubsection{Further training on predicted labels}

After the first training on the created labels, the model could distinguish classes and also separate foreground and background objects. To improve the model, its capability to create false positives that could be used as pseudo-labels for further training was tested. Using predictions of a model as labels for further training is the simplest method of the self-training paradigm, originally introduced as “pseudo-labeling’’ by Lee (2013)~\cite{lee_pseudo-label_nodate}.

Pseudo-labels had to be filtered by a confidence threshold to exclude actual false positives from not-yet-labeled target objects. The thresholds tested were derived from visual inspection. The main trade-off was allowing enough pseudo-labels to improve the model without degrading its predictions by the inclusion of wrong objects.

The first iteration transferred the rules learned on the augmented data to the original images with the background structures included. False positives consisted mainly of insects on the background structures, and the threshold had to be low because otherwise many insects on lines or letters remained unlabeled and the data would have contained contradicting information. Further iterations were used to enhance the quality of the predictions by using higher thresholds adapted to each class. The training length was set to 20 epochs for all runs.

Several unsuccessful experiments are not reported. These included higher confidence thresholds combined with shorter training runs for the initial pseudo-labels to prevent to many wrong lables. Adding new images to the training set, which were then completely pseudo-labeled, was also tested, as well as implementing a class-loss weighting based on the confidence of the predicted pseudo-labels.

\section{Results}

\subsection{Comparing supervised training and self-training}
\label{sec:final_results}
The best model was the supervised model trained on full images with the manual labels (S4). In validation it reached the target zone for all but the incoherently labeled thrips. Because confidence thresholds were derived from the incompletely labeled validation set, they did not provide the thresholds with the best performance for the model on the test set, where it missed the target zone for all but the leaf miner flies. However, as can be seen in the precision–recall curve on the test set, the model increased precision overall, because the more complete labels led to lower false positive rates.

The best self-trained model (7.2) was also trained on full images and reached the target zone only for the leaf miner flies.


\subsection{Evaluation settings}

All final tests were conducted with a threshold of 0.5 for filtering predictions contained in larger predicted boxes and the same threshold for allowing predictions contained inside ground truth boxes to count as true positives. For the tiles, a stride of 420 was used. These settings never degraded performance, but the scale of improvement differed between models. Tests with the supervised models S1 and S2 showed that changing the stride from 440 to 420 always brought a boost in the number of true positives. The extra containment filter before comparing the boxes brought a slight reduction in false negatives only for model S1. The extra containment step in the comparison to the ground truth with a threshold of 0.9 always brought benefits in all three metrics, but setting the threshold to 0.5 improved results only for stride 420.

\subsection{Self-training}



For both tiles and full images, the combination of augmented datasets 1 and 2, with the original images excluded, provided the best dataset for training. For tiles, the addition of empty tiles provided some improvements but, as reported above, training on full images yielded the best results. Compared to the labels created by image processing, training improved recall for all classes while retaining precision (compare the confusion matrices in Figure~\ref{fig:best_selftrained_conf} and Figure~\ref{fig:confusion_img_processing}).

After the first training on the augmented data, pseudo-labels on the original images consisted largely of insects on background structures that were missed during image processing. These were included in the first training iteration and helped the model to stabilize. Further iterations with new pseudo-labels brought only marginal improvements, with performance stagnating after two iterations for full images and after three iterations for tiles.

Visual inspection of the pseudo-labels showed no differentiation between insects and other objects based on confidence. Accordingly, visual inspection of the predictions of the best self-trained model (7.2) showed false positives for foliage and insect fragments. The inspection also revealed a larger number of still-unlabeled but correctly predicted fungus gnats and whiteflies in some images. Correct labeling of these objects would increase the number of true positives and decrease the number of false positives, which in turn would raise both precision and recall.

\section{Discussion}

The experiments demonstrate that data quality is the primary factor influencing model performance: For the leaf miner flies, which have clean images excluding any non-target objects, both supervised training on manual labels and self-training on generated labels led to models exceeding the expected precision and recall values. In contrast, the images for fungus gnats and whiteflies were of lower quality because they contained a higher number of insect fragments, which correlated to worse performance of the trained models. The reduced image quality affected both the manual labeling and the generation of labels by image-processing. For the thrips subset, the large number of objects that could not be clearly assigned to a class prevented successful training altogether.

For all but the leaf miner flies, the differences between the initial labels used for validation and the corrected labels used in the test set highlight the difficulty of achieving consistent separation between target and non-target objects in the manual labeling. This is further supported by the fact that even the corrected test set still contains inconsistencies and missing labels. Nevertheless, for fungus gnats and whiteflies, supervised training provided a model that reached the targeted metrics.

Also the image processing approach could not reliably distinguish between fragments and complete insects, as they often share simple features such as size and hue. As a result, models trained on the generated labels could not differentiate between correct and incorrect detections based on confidence. Therefore, confidence thresholding or weighting by confidence in pseudo-labeling were no viable solutions. Although data augmentation could mitigate the biases introduced by covering background structures and missing the darker whiteflies, the decision boundary implied by the image-processed and augmented labels could not be altered further through training, and the resulting predictions did not meet the expected metrics. The rapidly decreasing precision–recall curve in Figure~\ref{fig:best_self-trained} may also indicate an additional, yet unaddressed, bias introduced by the image processing.

With the supervised model achieving the best results, the experiments align with the state-of-the-art approach. They also support the claim that labeling is the main challenge in insect pest detection. Although the data was subdivided into subsets for the pest classes to simplify the process, the manual labels—and consequently the model performance—would still would benefit from collaborative review to improve consistency and completeness. The experiments also showed that for fungus gnats, leaf miner flies, and whiteflies, a balanced dataset containing about 6000 instances is already sufficient for training.

For further experiments, adaptations are possible at two stages. During image creation, resources can be saved by determining the necessary number of images beforehand. Effort could then be redirected toward improving image quality by adapting the catching method or selecting appropriate images before labeling. If data quality comparable to the leaf miner flies subset could be achieved, the tested alternative approach could replace manual labeling for the training data. Higher data quality would also make manual labeling easier and more efficient, allowing remaining resources to be invested in improving label quality. In the present case, labeling 6000 instances per class (and 12,000 for the thrips to adress the differences between males and females) corresponds to roughly half of all actually created labels (Table~\ref{tab:insect-stats}). Even with collaborative reviews for the more challenging insect types, there would still be enough resources for tasks such as preselecting suitable images.

The alternative approach based on image processing  has the potential to make the labeling process more efficient in large projects. Labeling software often supports model-based pre-labeling, for which the self-trained model presented here would be well suited, as manual labeling would often be reduced to simply adding or removing a small number of bounding boxes. For a single project, manual labeling is still more efficient than first developing a model for label prediction. However, for projects that aim to continuously expand the number of detectable pest classes, a tool for generating pseudo-labels would streamline the inclusion of new classes. Unlike supervised training, where labeling effort grows with each additional class, the development costs for such a tool occur only once. Likewise, the development costs for the image processing can be reduced in this context, because the generated labels only need to be sufficient for pre-labeling. Based on the presented results and with clean data comparable to those given for the leaf miner flies, the necessary model for pre-labeling can be created within a single workday. Also the adaptation to changes such as images with different resolutions or different backgrounds can be addressed in this setup.

In conclusion, the tested alternative approach is more sensitive to data quality than the state-of-the-art supervised approach. Lower data quality leads to higher development costs, since each problem requires an individual adaptation with specific thresholds. In contrast, supervised learning can compensate for lower data quality through more accurate labeling and produces sufficient results in less time and with comparatively low development costs. Nevertheless, an adapted version of the alternative approach may still facilitate the labeling process and is an option for long-term projects.
